# The organisation as a commitment and learning machine

## A design thesis for 2026 and beyond

An organisation coordinates commitments that extend beyond one person's capacity or tenure. A customer delivery, a public service obligation, a budget allocation, or a strategic decision creates expectations and dependencies. A commitment turns an intention into something others can reasonably act on.

Our thesis is that organisations should make this structure explicit and build technology around it. The commitment machine is a description of institutional coordination; its people are participants with judgment, rights, and interests. They are not components to optimise as interchangeable resources.

This framing follows [The Commitment Machine](https://www.reflective.se/signals/the-commitment-machine). It is a useful proposed abstraction, not a claim that every aspect of organisational life reduces to a measurable promise. Trust, care, exploration, informal cooperation, and unexpressed needs matter. We must test where the abstraction helps and where it obscures them. Some commitments are enduring duties without a deadline; the first prediction task deliberately covers only bounded, assessable commitments.

## Stop requiring humans to integrate the enterprise

Business systems each represent part of the work. Their local records rarely express the whole obligation: why it exists, what another team believes it means, which assumption makes it feasible, or who is authorised to change it. People repeatedly repair these gaps through meetings, spreadsheets, memory, and personal relationships.

The Organisation Core is the continuity that should survive those tools. It needs stable commitment identity, versioned meaning, explicit authority, dependencies, and evidence links. It does not require a single vendor or a database containing every organisational fact. Domain systems can retain authority for their records while contributing authenticated observations to a shared commitment history.

That changes application design. A sales app contributes a promise to the same organisational history that delivery, finance, and governance use. An app cannot quietly reinterpret the promise for its own workflow. A model cannot silently rewrite it to make its forecast look better.

## Three things that must remain different

| Question | Evidence needed | What it tells us |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Did we honour the commitment? | The accepted version, deadline, criteria, and observed fulfilment | The commitment gap |
| Was the forecast accurate? | A prediction made before the outcome and a settled label | Prediction error and calibration |
| Do we know enough to assess either? | Observation coverage, timing, disagreement, and provenance | The evidence gap |

A model that accurately predicts repeated failure has not improved the organisation. A useful intervention may prevent a predicted failure. A revised commitment may be responsible adaptation, while still imposing costs on its beneficiary. Evaluate these separately.

Never reward quiet deadline changes or classify missing evidence as success. Never assume cancellation is always failure or that keeping an obsolete promise is always desirable. The organisation must judge whether its commitments remain worth keeping as well as whether it can keep them.

## Learning has a consequence

A learning loop preserves what was promised and expected, what was done, what happened, and how the organisation responded. It also tests the response later. A retrospective becomes organisational learning when it changes a future assumption, coordination practice, resource decision, or governing rule in a way that can be examined.

Learning from priors begins before model training. Retrieving comparable cases, examining base rates, and checking explicit rules can already inform a decision. A prior is a revisable expectation with provenance and a scope of applicability. In Bayesian models it may be a formal prior distribution; elsewhere we use the term more broadly and name the actual method.

Learning also includes changing the framing. Repeated failure might reveal an unrealistic sales policy, a mistaken success criterion, or a commitment nobody should have made. A predictor trained only to improve completion rates could reinforce those errors.

## Collective intelligence must preserve human capacity

[Human Judgment](https://www.reflective.se/signals/human-judgment) places accountable judgment at the centre of an increasingly automated organisation. [System 3 Should Grow System 2](https://www.reflective.se/signals/system-3-should-grow-system-2) argues for AI-supported deliberation that develops human capability.

We adopt that as a design objective. “System 2b” is Reflective's working term for structured human–AI co-deliberation, not an established scientific category. More automation does not, by itself, prove better reasoning or greater understanding.

Applications should make assumptions explorable, preserve dissent, show uncertainty, and support challenges. Delegation can be extensive within explicit authority. For consequential decisions, measure whether participants can explain the tradeoff, recognise an invalid assumption, and transfer understanding to a new case. Clicking approval is insufficient evidence.

Personal practice and recall history are separate from organisational outcomes. They must not become employee rankings or default training inputs. The learning unit here is a commitment trajectory situated in an initiative and dependency network.

## What we should be willing to discover

The commitment abstraction might be too expensive to maintain in some settings. Better records might help people while adding little predictive value. A small statistical model might outperform a learned representation. Cross-organisation transfer might fail. Advice might improve delivery while weakening understanding.

Each is an informative result. The purpose is a more capable organisation, and the research method must permit the architecture to change.
